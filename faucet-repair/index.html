Honest start

You may fix a faucet yourself, and here is where that ends

Oklahoma statute section 1017 exempts minor repairs, such as faucets and fixture parts, from state plumbing licensing, and state law lets an individual do plumbing work on their own residence. So replacing a cartridge or washer is a legitimate DIY job. If you replace instead of repair, look for the EPA WaterSense label on a new faucet, which marks models that use less water.

DIY ends where the water you cannot shut off begins. A stuck stop valve, a leaking supply line inside a wall, or any repair that needs the main shut off is a call. The same goes if you are not sure which parts you are holding.

Fixing a dripping faucet in the right order

This sequence works for most single-handle and two-handle faucets.

1

Shut the water

Close both stop valves under the sink. Open the faucet to drain pressure and confirm the water is off.

2

Plug the drain

A rag in the sink drain keeps small screws from vanishing. Pry the cap off the handle and remove the screw beneath.

3

Pull the cartridge or stem

Lift the handle and unscrew the retaining nut. Pull out the cartridge, or the stem on a compression faucet.

4

Match the part

Take the old part to a hardware store. Brand and model matter, since cartridges are rarely interchangeable.

5

Reassemble and flush

Rebuild in reverse, then remove the aerator and run water to flush debris before reinstalling it.

Supply side

Broken supply valve: stop the water first

The shut-off valves under a sink or behind a washer fail more often than people expect. A valve that has not been turned in years can seize, drip at the stem, or snap when forced. When one breaks, the line is under full pressure, so the main shut-off is the safe move.

If a faucet has to be replaced rather than repaired, look for the EPA WaterSense label on bathroom models, which sets a flow standard of 1.5 gallons per minute. Any faucet that supplies drinking water should also carry NSF/ANSI 61 and 372 marking, which covers the federal lead-free requirement.

Stop valve under the sink

Each fixture has a small valve on its own supply line. Turn it clockwise to close. If it will not move, leaks at the stem or sprays when touched, do not wrench it, since the stem can break loose.

Angle stop

The angle stop is the quarter-turn or multi-turn valve where the line comes out of the wall and turns to the fixture. Old multi-turn stops wear their packing and washers. Replacing one means shutting off the house water, which is why many people call for this one.

Washing machine valve

The two hot and cold valves behind the washer are easy to ignore. A drip at the hose, a stuck handle, or a burst braided hose can flood a laundry room fast. Turn the valves off when you leave for a trip, and replace rubber hoses with braided ones.

Bath and shower

Shower, tub and drain trouble

Bathroom fixtures hide their problems behind tile and in the wall, so the symptom matters.

Leaking shower valve or showerhead

A showerhead that drips after shutoff usually has a worn washer or a loose arm connection. A valve that leaks inside the wall, or a stained ceiling below, is not a DIY. Try tightening the head with plumber tape first.

Clogged shower or tub drain

Hair and soap form a plug just below the strainer. A drain tool pulls most of it out. If water backs up in a second fixture or a floor drain, the clog is further down, and drain cleaning is the answer.

Bathtub faucet repair

Tub spouts and the diverter that sends water to the shower wear out together. Water that dribbles from the spout while the shower runs means a failing diverter. Most tub faucets use the same cartridge style as a sink.

General faucet repair

Kitchen and bath faucets share causes: worn seals, mineral scale, loose supply nuts. Spotting which one you have saves guessing.

What to do before the plumber arrives

Do these while you wait.

Close the stop valve

Turn the oval handle under the fixture clockwise. If it will not close, shut the main.

Protect the cabinet

Empty the cabinet under the sink and set a bucket under the leak.

Locate the main

Know where the main water valve sits so you can shut it in seconds.

Photograph the fixture

A photo of the brand name and the damage helps match parts and supports an insurance claim.
