Sewer Camera Inspection in Jenks, OK

A sewer camera inspection in Jenks, Oklahoma sends a waterproof camera on a flexible rod through your main line so a licensed plumber can see the inside of the pipe on a screen. It answers the question a cable cannot: what is actually wrong down there.

Homeowners in Tulsa County use it before buying a house, after a repeat clog, or to find where the line runs before digging. Call to ask about timing, and we give you a price before work starts.

What the camera shows

What a sewer camera finds in the pipe

The head carries a light and a lens, and the plumber watches the screen as it advances.

Roots

Fine hairs or thick masses entering at a joint, which shows where water is getting in and how far they reach.

Sags and bellies

A low spot where water pools instead of draining, often seen as standing water in the picture.

Cracks and breaks

Lines along the pipe, missing sections or a collapse that stops the camera.

Offset joints

Pieces that no longer line up, which catch paper and invite roots.

Buildup

A coating of grease, scale or sludge that narrows the pipe.

Why people schedule it

Buying a home, or a drain that keeps backing up

A house inspection normally does not include the buried sewer lateral. Census data shows about 16% of Jenks homes were built before 1980, so a buyer eyeing one of the older pockets near downtown may want to see clay or cast iron before closing. For a newer home, the camera still catches builder-installed low spots, offset joints or construction debris.

The other common reason is a line that backs up on repeat. Clearing it with a cable gets water moving, and the camera finds why it stopped. See sewer line cleaning for how the line is opened first.

Locating the line

A camera with a transmitter in the head lets a locator at the surface trace the pipe and its depth. That helps you avoid it when planning a patio, fence post or addition, and helps the plumber dig the right spot for a repair.

What a recording does and does not prove

A video is strong evidence, but it is not a guarantee.

What it shows well

  • Roots, cracks and offsets visible on camera
  • Standing water in a sag at the time of the run
  • Where the pipe turns, joins or ends
  • Condition of the pipe up to the camera's reach

What it cannot tell you

  • Whether a root will cause a backup next year
  • Anything past a blockage or collapse that stops the camera
  • A hairline crack that looks clean on video
  • Who legally owns the section, which is a question for the city

Treat the recording as a snapshot. It is the best view available of a buried pipe, and it should be read alongside the age and material of the home.

What happens on inspection day

A typical visit is short and nothing is dug up.

1

Reach the line

The plumber uses a cleanout where there is one, or pulls a toilet or enters through a vent if there is not.

2

Run the camera

The rod is fed in toward the street while the plumber watches and notes distance to each feature.

3

Mark and locate

Where something is found, a locator at the surface marks it so a repair can be planned.

4

Review the picture

You see the screen, hear what was found and ask questions before any repair is discussed.

Camera before cleaning, or after

On a clog that has not been cleared, the camera may not get through. On a clear line, a quick run first shows whether jetting is safe. Many plumbers clear the line first, then film, so ask which order fits your situation.

What affects inspection cost

Price depends on access and length, and the plumber gives you the number before the camera goes in.

AccessA cleanout makes the run simple, and a toilet pull or roof vent adds labor.
Line lengthA long run to the street takes more time than a short one.
LocatingTracing the line with a transmitter and marking depth adds a step.
Cleaning firstIf the pipe is blocked, it must be cleared before the camera can pass.
Recording and reportAsk what you get to keep, such as the video file, and whether the plumber will walk you through it.

Insurance seldom pays for an inspection, though a repair after a sudden covered loss may be considered. Policies differ, so read yours.
