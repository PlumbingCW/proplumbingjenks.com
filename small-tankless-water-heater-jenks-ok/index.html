Small Tankless Water Heaters: What Fits in a Jenks, OK Home

A small tankless water heater works best at one fixture, like a distant bathroom sink or a kitchen sink, where waiting for hot water wastes time. It heats water only while a tap is open, using an electric element or a small gas burner.

A shower is possible, but flow rate and incoming water temperature decide it. This guide shows how to size one and when a bigger unit is the better buy.

Start with the fixture, since flow rate drives everything else.

Check the product label for gallons per minute and the temperature rise it can deliver.

Flow rate and temperature rise decide everything

Every tankless heater has two numbers: how many gallons per minute it can move, and how many degrees it can add. A bathroom faucet commonly runs about 1 to 1.5 gallons per minute, and a kitchen faucet near 1.5. A showerhead often uses 2 or more.

Say the incoming water is 55 degrees in January and you want 105 at the tap. That is a 50 degree rise at your flow rate. A small unit that can only add 35 degrees will deliver warm water at best, so check the manufacturer's rise chart for the coldest month.

Aerators matter here. Swapping in a lower-flow aerator can raise the temperature a small unit delivers, because slower water has more time to heat. A plumber can check what your fixture actually flows with a bucket and a timer, which beats trusting the number on the box.

Checks before you buy one

Run through these before you order. Each one has caught buyers off guard.

  • ✓Voltage. Small 120-volt units suit a hand-washing sink. Showers and kitchens usually call for 240-volt units on a dedicated circuit.
  • ✓Panel space. A 240-volt unit needs a breaker slot and proper wire gauge. Have a licensed professional check your panel.
  • ✓Water quality. Scale builds on the heating element or the heat exchanger, and descaling matters for lifespan.
  • ✓Placement. Under a sink is fine, but an unheated garage or exterior wall can freeze in winter.
  • ✓Shutoff and relief. Local code and the manual may require isolation valves or a relief valve on the unit.

Electric or gas for a small unit

Small units are almost all electric, because a gas burner needs a vent and a gas line at the fixture. Electric point-of-use heaters have no exhaust and install close to the tap. Gas models are larger and belong in a utility space with proper venting.

If you are weighing a bigger system for the whole home, the tankless water heater page covers full-size units and service. For a single sink, a small electric unit usually costs far less to buy and install.

Why a small unit and not a bigger tank

A mini-tank stores two to six gallons and recovers quickly, but it holds the same water all day. A tankless unit heats on demand, so it adds no standby loss. It cannot supply a second fixture at the same moment, however.

Homes in Bixby and Sapulpa often ask about this setup for a bonus room or a detached garage bath, since a long pipe run from the main heater leaves the tap cold for a minute.

If hot water already arrives fast at that fixture, you do not need one. A small unit fixes the wait, not a weak main heater. A leaking or slow main tank is a job for water heater repair first.

Cold-water arrival and delay

Even with a tankless unit, any water in the pipe between the heater and the tap is cold at first. Placing the unit within a few feet of the fixture shortens that wait. A plumbing installation visit can also check pipe size and shutoffs.

Local note

Jenks, Oklahoma: winter cold and your small unit

NWS Tulsa climate normals for 1991 to 2020 show a January average low near 28 degrees and about 72 days a year at or below 32 degrees. That is a Tulsa station reading, not a Jenks gauge, but it tells you a unit in a garage or an exterior-wall cabinet faces real freeze risk. Keep a small heater on an interior wall and insulate its supply lines.

The City of Tulsa also publishes an average hardness of about 8.2 grains per gallon for the Jewell plant that supplies Jenks, and the Jenks water report lists none. Scale can coat a tankless element, so ask the maker how often to descale and test your own tap for a better number.

Signs a small unit is not enough

These patterns point to a larger system or a different layout.

Water turns lukewarm

The unit cannot add enough degrees at your flow rate, especially in winter.

Cold bursts while running

Heater shuts down when flow drops below its minimum, or when a second tap opens.

Breaker trips

The circuit is undersized for the element, so stop and have a licensed professional look.

Whole-house plans

Two bathrooms running together need a full-size unit, not three small ones.
